# Voice Guide

Source: distilled from local career reference materials and public writing samples on 2026-08-05.

Use this when drafting site copy, portfolio case studies, resume language, and future blog migration notes.

Public writing sources reviewed:

- Professional education blog: `https://marklwright.com/`
- Personal travel blog: `https://markwright.blogspot.com/`
- Personal political/opinion blog: `https://markismexplored.blogspot.com/`

Blockquotes and quoted source excerpts were excluded from voice analysis.

## Tone

Reflective, teacherly, analytical, and quietly personal. Explain why an idea matters before selling the outcome. Sound confident without becoming promotional.

The voice is comfortable with judgment. It often names a tension, says what does not quite work, and then moves toward a practical alternative. It should not sound artificially neutral when a clear point of view would be more honest.

## Rhythm

Mix short declarative sentences with longer reflective sentences. Build an argument through rhythm and sequence instead of packing every line with credentials.

The informal writing often uses a setup-and-turn rhythm: describe the plan, admit the complication, then explain what actually happened. Preserve that human sequence in portfolio writing when it fits.

## Openings

Start from a concrete belief, tension, memory, or observation, then move into experience. Avoid generic fit statements and role-summary language.

Good openings can begin with a practical problem: a course that had to be made usable, a system that created friction, a group that needed guidance, or an assumption that did not survive contact with the classroom.

## Vocabulary

Prefer grounded words such as work, use, practice, teacher, learner, classroom, guidance, structure, fit, moment, and what happens. Use technical terms when they clarify the work.

Education writing can use research and theory, but it should translate them back into human terms: what teachers do, what learners notice, what the system makes easier or harder.

## Structure

Move from observation to example to lesson. Let the answer reason through a problem. Strong copy should feel like a short professional reflection, not a polished marketing paragraph.

For portfolio case studies, prefer this shape:

1. What was happening.
2. Why the obvious answer was not enough.
3. What I tried or built.
4. What changed, or what I learned.
5. What I would carry forward.

## First Person

First person is natural when appropriate. Phrases like "I learned," "I found," "my role has been," and "what matters to me" fit the voice.

The travel writing is especially comfortable with plain self-correction: "I thought this would be simple; it was not." Use that honesty when describing process, tradeoffs, and lessons learned.

## Humor and Warmth

Small, dry humor fits. It usually appears as understatement, a quick aside, or a frank admission that the plan and the reality were not the same thing. Keep it light on professional pages, but do not sand it away completely.

## Argument Style

The opinion writing builds from a concrete claim, tests the logic, and is willing to call out bad framing. For public professional pages, keep the same clarity but soften the heat. The useful pattern is: name the assumption, show the consequence, offer the better frame.

## Site Copy Guidance

The site should sound like a person who has actually worked in classrooms, learning systems, and messy operational environments. Favor concrete nouns and lived examples over abstract brand language.

Do not make every paragraph inspirational. It is better to be clear, useful, and a little reflective.

## Avoid

- Generic application language.
- Over-polished transitions.
- Long lists of resume keywords in prose.
- "Unique blend."
- "Intersection of."
- "Passionate about."
- "Multiplier effect" unless unpacked concretely.
- Empty thought-leadership language.
- Copy that hides every tradeoff or complication.
- Borrowed phrasing from quoted authors, articles, or blockquotes.
